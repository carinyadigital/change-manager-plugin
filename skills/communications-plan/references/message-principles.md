# Message principles

The plugin's own guidance for planning and writing change communications. Used
by `communications-plan` (what to say, who says it, when) and `comms-draft`
(how to write it).

## 1. Answer first

Open with what is happening and what the reader needs to do. Background and
reasons follow. If the reader stops after the first two sentences, they should
still know the main point and their next step.

## 2. Plain English

- Short sentences, everyday words, active voice ("we will move", not "a move
  will be undertaken").
- No project names, acronyms or internal jargon without a plain explanation.
- Say "you" to the reader and "we" for the organisation.
- Be specific about dates, places and actions. Vague messages create rumour.
- Do not oversell. Describe benefits the business has actually committed to,
  and be honest about what will be harder at first.

## 3. The core narrative

Every message draws on the same six answers, so people hear one consistent
story:

1. **Why** — the business reason, in a sentence or two.
2. **What** — what is changing.
3. **When** — the key dates.
4. **What it means for me** — what changes in this group's daily work.
5. **What is not changing** — people worry about this as much as the change.
6. **Where to get help** — a named route, not "contact the project".

If an answer is not yet decided, say so and say when it will be.

## 4. Who should send what

People want to hear different things from different people.

| Message type | Best sender | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why the organisation is changing, the direction, the business reasons | The most senior leader accountable for the change (sponsor) | Signals the change is a priority and will be backed |
| What it means for my team and my job | The direct manager or supervisor | They know the work and people trust them on personal impact |
| How to do the new thing — dates, training, systems, steps | Project team, trainers, champions | They have the detail and can answer follow-up questions |
| Local reactions, questions, early wins | Champions, team leaders | Peer voice; close to the work |

Avoid sending personal-impact news from a central mailbox. Avoid having the
project team announce strategic reasons.

## 5. Sequence

- **Leaders first.** Brief leaders and managers, with a pack and time for
  their questions, before their teams hear.
- **In person before broadcast** for anything that changes someone's role,
  location, hours, pay or job security. The affected people hear first, from
  their manager, with HR support in place.
- **Repeat across channels.** People need to hear an important message more
  than once and in more than one way before it sticks.
- **Fill the quiet.** During long design phases, send short updates on what
  is decided and what is not.

## 6. Two-way by design

Every audience needs a way to ask questions and be heard: manager huddles,
Q&A sessions, an FAQ that is updated, a monitored inbox, a short pulse survey,
champion feedback. Close the loop by telling people what you heard and what
changed as a result.

## 7. Sensitive topics

Role changes, redundancy, relocation, pay, hours, performance and anything
legal or industrial need HR and, where relevant, legal or employee-relations
review before drafting goes further. Consultation obligations may apply
depending on jurisdiction and agreements. The plugin flags these; it does not
give legal advice.
